What Is Opera (And Why It Still Matters)

June 09, 2025 Opera Shalem · 3 min read

A beginner's guide to understanding opera as an art form and cultural force.

At Opera Shalem, we're often asked: What exactly is opera? Is it grand spectacle with powdered wigs? Is it long and difficult? Is it still relevant in today's cultural landscape?

For us, the answer is simple: opera is one of the most powerful storytelling tools in human history. But to understand why opera matters today, it helps to understand where this art form comes from and how it has evolved.

A Brief History: From Renaissance to Modern Stage

Opera as we know it began around 1600 in Italy, born from the Renaissance fascination with reviving ancient Greek drama. Composers like Claudio Monteverdi experimented with combining music, poetry, and theater to heighten emotional expression. The earliest operas, like L'Orfeo (1607), already revealed opera's unique power: music can deepen and extend what words alone cannot say.

The genre spread rapidly across Europe. Handel brought Italian-style opera to London in the early 18th century. By the 19th century, opera had become a global cultural force. Verdi in Italy, Wagner in Germany, and Bizet in France each brought their national flavor to this art form.

Today's opera companies continue this tradition while adapting to contemporary audiences through innovative staging, multimedia integration, and diverse casting approaches.

What Makes Opera 'Opera'?

At its core, opera is theatrical storytelling through music. It combines multiple art forms—text (libretto), composition, staging, design, movement, and voice—into a single, immersive experience. It's not 'just' music. And it's not 'just' theater. It is both. At once.

The Spectrum of Musical Theater

Traditionally, opera is sung throughout, but many works use recitative—a speech-like style of singing—to move the plot along, alternating with more musically elaborate arias and ensembles. Some operas even include spoken dialogue, bridging the gap between opera and musical theater.

French opéra comique, Italian dramma giocoso, and many of Mozart's works fall into this hybrid territory. These pieces—Mozart's Don Giovanni, Rossini's Il barbiere di Siviglia—are considered part of the opera canon, yet structurally resemble operetta.

Over time, distinctions between opera and operetta hardened, often for institutional reasons: different theaters, different audiences, different funding models. Today's split between opera companies and musical theater reflects similar forces. But artistically, the lines are—and have always been—fluid.

Why Opera Still Speaks

A Quiet Revival in Popular Culture

Opera has sometimes been dismissed as elitist or outdated. But something surprising is happening: opera is having a quiet revival in mainstream entertainment.

In recent years, streaming series like Bridgerton have reintroduced classical vocal music to millions of viewers. That show's score, blending Baroque string music with contemporary pop arrangements, has sparked renewed interest in the original material and live performances.

From Opera Houses to Hollywood

The connection between opera and popular culture runs deeper than many realize. During the 1930s and 1940s, many Jewish and dissident composers fled persecution and found refuge in America, particularly in Hollywood. These classically trained musicians helped develop the sweeping, emotionally powerful sound that became synonymous with film scoring.

Today, much of the great 'operatic' music that audiences experience happens in movie theaters. Film soundtracks have become hugely popular—Hans Zimmer concerts sell out arenas, and live orchestral performances of Lord of the Rings, Star Wars, and Harry Potter scores draw massive audiences. This represents a continuation of opera's core mission: using music to heighten dramatic storytelling.

Opera Shalem's Vision

At Opera Shalem, we're not just reviving opera. We're redefining it as an inclusive, rooted, resonant Jewish art form. We take opera seriously as a tradition, but we don't treat it as a museum piece.

Most opera companies focus exclusively on the classical canon: Mozart, Puccini, Verdi. Some occasionally touch operetta. Fewer still embrace musicals. But we see the full spectrum—from Baroque to Broadway—as fertile ground for Jewish cultural expression.

What We're Building

Our vision includes works from the established operatic canon approached with fresh dramaturgical perspective, new Jewish operas that speak to historical and contemporary experience, operetta and musical theater reimagined as part of the Israeli cultural fabric, and intercultural collaboration with artists across traditions and genres.

Opera is uniquely capable of expressing the unspeakable. That's why it's endured across centuries and cultures. And that's why we believe it's the right form for the Jewish stories we need to tell now.

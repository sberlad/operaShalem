Repertoire Planning: How Opera Companies Choose What to Perform

March 01, 2026 Opera Shalem · 5 min read

The artistic, financial, and cultural considerations behind season programming.

The Art and Science of Season Planning

Every season, opera companies worldwide make similar decisions: which works to perform, in what order, with which artists, at what budget. These choices shape what audiences experience, which artists get opportunities, and which works enter or remain in the active repertoire.

For emerging companies like Opera Shalem, repertoire planning is especially critical. Our early seasons will establish artistic identity, build audience, and demonstrate viability. These aren't just programming decisions—they're strategic institutional development.

The Multiple Factors

Artistic Vision vs. Box Office Reality

The tension between artistic ambition and financial sustainability drives every programming decision. The Metropolitan Opera can afford experimental programming because subscriptions and endowment provide financial cushion. Smaller companies face harder trade-offs.

Popular works like Puccini's La bohème or Bizet's Carmen sell tickets but don't distinguish a company artistically. Rarely-performed baroque opera or contemporary premieres demonstrate adventurous programming but risk smaller audiences.

Smart programming balances both: anchor seasons with box-office draws while including works that define artistic identity.

Casting Constraints

Repertoire choices depend heavily on available artists. Ensemble companies program around their resident artists' voices and strengths. Project-based companies must consider which artists they can attract and afford.

A company with a strong dramatic soprano might program Tosca, Turandot, or Strauss. Without that voice type, those works become harder to cast well.

For Opera Shalem's ensemble model, this works differently. We'll build the ensemble first, then program to showcase their strengths while pushing artistic growth.

Production Costs

Wagner's Ring Cycle requires large orchestra, chorus, multiple specialized singers, complex staging, and extensive rehearsal time. Rossini's Il barbiere di Siviglia needs smaller forces and less rehearsal. These cost differences shape programming choices.

Orchestra size matters enormously. Mozart requires 30-40 musicians, Verdi 60-70, Wagner 90-100. Smaller orchestras mean lower costs but limit repertoire options.

Set and costume budgets vary wildly. Minimalist productions save money but risk looking cheap. Elaborate productions attract audiences but strain budgets.

Audience Development

Programming shapes audience over time. Companies performing only standard repertoire attract opera enthusiasts but not new audiences. Companies performing only contemporary work appeal to adventurous listeners but may alienate traditionalists.

Successful audience development requires strategic programming across multiple seasons:

Entry Points: Works that welcome opera newcomers: Mozart's Die Zauberflöte, Humperdinck's Hänsel und Gretel, Bernstein's Candide. Accessible stories, beautiful music, reasonable length.

Depth Building: Once audiences trust the company, introduce more challenging works: Janáček's Jenůfa, Britten's Peter Grimes, Berg's Wozzeck.

Completeness: Over time, present comprehensive views of major composers: not just Puccini's hits but also La rondine or Le villi.

The Canon vs. Discovery

Opera repertoire divides roughly into tiers:

Core Canon: 30-40 works performed globally hundreds of times annually: Verdi, Puccini, Mozart, Wagner standards. Operabase statistics show these works dominating worldwide programming.

Extended Repertoire: 100-150 works performed regularly but less frequently: lesser-known Verdi, Handel operas, French grand opera, bel canto rarities.

Rediscoveries: Hundreds of works rarely performed but artistically valuable: baroque opera, 19th-century French works, early 20th-century modernism.

Contemporary: New commissions and recent compositions building future repertoire.

Programming decisions determine which works audiences know. When companies repeatedly program Puccini over contemporary composers, they shape cultural narratives about what opera is.

Thematic Programming

Smart companies build thematic connections across seasons:

Composer Focus: A Verdi season exploring his artistic development, or complete Mozart-Da Ponte cycle.

Historical Periods: Baroque opera season, or survey of opera between World Wars.

Dramatic Themes: Works exploring power, love, betrayal, or redemption across different styles and periods.

Cultural Connections: For Opera Shalem, this might mean seasons connecting Jewish musical traditions with operatic canon, or works exploring Jerusalem across centuries.

Language Considerations

Should opera be performed in original languages with supertitles, or translated into local language?

International practice favors original languages. Musical phrasing is written for specific languages. Translation often distorts vocal lines and poetic meaning.

But accessibility matters. Supertitles help, but some audiences find them distracting. Translation can make works more immediately comprehensible.

For Hebrew-speaking Israel, this creates interesting questions. Should Italian opera be performed in Italian with Hebrew supertitles, or translated into Hebrew? What about creating new Hebrew-language operatic works alongside translated repertoire?

Contemporary Commissioning

Programming new works serves multiple purposes:

Artistic Vitality: Opera remains a living art form only if new works enter repertoire regularly.

Cultural Relevance: Contemporary composers can address current issues and experiences that 19th-century works cannot.

Distinctive Identity: World premieres distinguish companies artistically and attract media attention.

Future Canon: Today's commissions become tomorrow's standard repertoire—if they're good enough.

For Opera Shalem, commissioning works that engage with Jewish experience, Israeli reality, and Jerusalem's significance will be essential to our mission.

Operetta and Musical Theater

Opera companies increasingly program operetta and musical theater. The line between these forms is historically fluid. Mozart's The Magic Flute and Rossini's The Barber of Seville include spoken dialogue, structurally resembling operetta more than grand opera.

Bernstein's West Side Story and Candide are performed by opera companies worldwide. Sondheim's works increasingly appear on opera stages. Kurt Weill's works bridge opera, operetta, and musical theater seamlessly.

This expansion serves audiences well, demonstrating that opera exists on a spectrum rather than in rigid categories. Matt Austerklein's work on Jewish musicology explores how these genre boundaries evolved and why they matter culturally.

Opera Shalem's Repertoire Vision

Our programming philosophy balances several principles:

Canonical Foundation: Core repertoire performed at highest level, demonstrating we can deliver Mozart, Verdi, and Puccini excellently.

Jewish Voice: Regular programming of operetta, musical theater, and contemporary works that engage Jewish experience and Israeli reality.

Discovery: Rarely-performed works that deserve renewed attention, whether baroque opera, French grand opera, or 20th-century modernism.

Commission Strategy: New works by Israeli and international composers developing authentic Jewish operatic voice.

Educational Mission: Programming that introduces new audiences to opera while serving experienced opera-goers.

The Long View

Season planning isn't isolated decision-making. It's long-term institutional strategy:

Years 1-3: Establish competence and build audience. Core repertoire plus strategic discoveries. Demonstrate ensemble quality.

Years 4-7: Expand artistic ambition. Introduce more challenging repertoire, contemporary commissions, fuller operetta programming.

Years 8-10: Achieve institutional maturity. Present complete operatic spectrum from baroque to contemporary, establish commissioning tradition, tour internationally.

This long-term thinking distinguishes permanent companies from project-based operations. We're not planning seasons—we're building an artistic institution that will serve Jerusalem for generations.

Smart repertoire planning shapes cultural narratives, develops audiences, showcases artists, and establishes institutional identity. For Opera Shalem, it's how we make good on our promise to build Jerusalem's permanent operatic voice.

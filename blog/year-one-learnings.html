What We Learned in Year One: Reflections on Building Cultural Infrastructure

December 15, 2025 Opera Shalem · 6 min read

Reflections on Opera Shalem's first year: challenges, pivots, and unexpected insights.

The Pivot We Didn't Expect

When Opera Shalem launched in 2024, we imagined a straightforward path: raise seed funding, incorporate legally, hire core staff, and open in 2027. Simple, direct, achievable.

Reality proved more complex. Building permanent cultural infrastructure in Jerusalem—Israel's first professional ensemble opera company since 1982—requires different thinking than launching a startup or producing a single season.

A year later, we're still in seed phase. We haven't opened. We haven't hired a full permanent staff. We haven't broken ground on a facility.

And that's actually good news.

What We Got Wrong

Underestimating Timeline

We assumed cultural infrastructure could be built on startup timelines. Raise money, execute plan, launch product.

But Opera Shalem isn't a product. It's an institution. The Metropolitan Opera took decades to achieve stability. European opera houses developed over centuries. Even successful recent opera companies like Oper Frankfurt required years of patient institutional building.

Permanent cultural infrastructure isn't built quickly because it requires:

Deep community relationships: Trust from donors, artists, audiences, and cultural leaders develops through consistent engagement over time, not quick campaigns.

Institutional expertise: Operating a permanent ensemble requires specialized knowledge in labor contracts, union relationships, facility management, and season planning. This expertise must be developed or acquired carefully.

Financial sustainability: Quick launches often mean quick collapses. Patient capital development ensures long-term viability.

Missing the Moment's Complexity

We launched during relatively stable times. Then October 7, 2023 happened. Israel entered existential war. By February 2026, the US and Israel had killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, triggering full-scale regional conflict.

Meanwhile, New York elected a DSA socialist mayor who supports Palestinian liberation—the same month Israel fights Hezbollah across Lebanon. The political environment for cultural fundraising grew exponentially more complex.

We didn't anticipate having to navigate building Jewish cultural institutions while Israel fights for survival and global antisemitism surges. That reality demanded strategic recalibration.

Undervaluing Patient Capital

Our initial fundraising approach emphasized urgent timelines and immediate impact. But the donors who build lasting institutions think differently. They invest in decades-long vision, not quarterly results.

This required complete mindset shift: from startup urgency to institutional patience, from project funding to endowment thinking, from transactional asks to partnership building.

What We Got Right

Institutional Before Individual

We resisted pressure to produce shows before building institutional foundation. Many suggested: "Just do a production! Show what you can do!"

But one-off productions don't build permanent infrastructure. They might generate buzz but won't create the ensemble model we're committed to. We stayed focused on institutional development rather than individual events.

Quality Over Speed

We could have rushed to launch with inadequate funding, compromised artistic standards, or unsustainable business models. Instead, we chose to build correctly even if that means building slowly.

This meant:

Thorough feasibility studies: Understanding what sustainable opera actually costs, what facilities actually require, what ensemble size actually makes sense.

Legal structure done right: Proper incorporation, governance, compliance—boring but essential.

Team development: Bringing together experienced leadership who understand both opera and institutional sustainability.

Transparency About Phase

We stopped pretending we were further along than reality. Seed phase is seed phase. Institutional development is institutional development. Being honest about where we actually are builds trust more than false projections.

Our communication shifted from "Join us for opening night!" to "Help us build something permanent." Turns out people respond better to honest partnership than polished marketing.

Key Learnings

Patience Is Strategic, Not Passive

Taking time doesn't mean doing nothing. It means doing the right things in the right order:

Building relationships before asking for money. Donors who understand our vision become partners, not transactions.

Developing expertise before making commitments. Understanding union contracts, facility requirements, and operational costs before signing anything prevents costly mistakes.

Establishing governance before hiring. Board structure, artistic oversight, and institutional checks matter before day-to-day operations begin.

Context Matters Enormously

Building Jewish cultural infrastructure in 2026 requires acknowledging geopolitical reality. Israel is at war. Antisemitism is rising globally. Jewish artists face systematic exclusion from cultural institutions.

This context doesn't make Opera Shalem less necessary—it makes it more necessary. But it does require acknowledging that cultural institution-building happens within political reality, not separate from it.

Mission Clarity Matters More Than Timeline

What matters isn't when we open but how we open. Opening in 2027 with shaky finances and uncertain sustainability would be worse than opening in 2029 with solid institutional foundation.

We're not building a company. We're building infrastructure meant to last generations. That requires patient, careful development.

What Comes Next

Completing Seed Phase

We're focused on reaching our $250K seed phase goal to fund:

Core team salaries: CEO, Artistic Director, Development Director working full-time on institutional development.

Legal and governance: Proper incorporation, board development, compliance structure.

Feasibility studies: Detailed analysis of facility requirements, ensemble size, repertoire planning, and financial modeling.

Partnership development: Building relationships with Jerusalem Academy of Music and Dance, municipal government, cultural organizations, and potential donors.

Architectural Vision

We're developing clearer vision for our eventual facility. Our conceptual rendering shows a building that honors Jerusalem's architectural heritage while providing world-class performance infrastructure.

But we're not rushing to build. First comes financial sustainability, proven operational model, and community support. Then comes brick and mortar.

Artist Relationships

We're building relationships with singers, conductors, directors, and designers who share our vision. These relationships matter more than contracts. When we're ready to hire our ensemble, we want artists who've grown with the vision, not just responded to job postings.

Educational Programming

Even before full operations begin, we can engage audiences through educational programming, lectures, and community events. Building audience happens over time, not overnight.

Advice to Future Cultural Entrepreneurs

If you're thinking about building cultural infrastructure:

Think institutional, not project. One successful production doesn't create sustainability. Permanent infrastructure requires different thinking.

Accept long timelines. If it's worth building, it's worth building right. Patient development serves mission better than rushed launches.

Find partners, not just donors. People who believe in long-term vision matter more than people writing one-time checks.

Be honest about phase. Seed phase is legitimate. Development phase is valuable work. Don't pretend you're further along than reality.

Study successful models. German ensemble opera works because of centuries of institutional development. Learn from what succeeds elsewhere.

Stay flexible on tactics, firm on mission. How we build may change. What we're building doesn't.

Why We're More Confident Now

A year ago, we had enthusiasm and vision. Today we have something more valuable: clarity.

We understand what we're building and why it matters. We know what sustainable opera actually requires. We've connected with partners who share long-term vision. We've developed institutional expertise that will serve us for decades.

Most importantly, we've accepted that building something permanent takes time—and that's not weakness but wisdom.

Opera Shalem will open. Jerusalem will have its permanent ensemble opera company. But it will happen when institutional foundation is solid, not when arbitrary timelines demand.

For those who've supported us through this learning year: thank you. Your patience and partnership make permanent cultural infrastructure possible. We're building something that will outlast all of us, and that requires the collective commitment you're demonstrating.

Year two begins with clear eyes, realistic timelines, and deeper commitment than ever. The work continues.
